CHAPTER IV 
PRESENTATION, ANALYSIS AND INTERPRETATION OF DATA 
In this chapter, the researchers present an in-depth analysis of the study’s results, providing 
a clear summary of the collected data and its significance. The data are organized in tables for 
clarity, accompanied by a concise discussion that highlights their relevance and implications. 
Profile of the respondents 
The demographic profile of respondents includes key details relevant to household energy 
consumption in Mandaue City and Lapu -Lapu City. The profile captures the respondents’ city of 
residence, type of residence, responsibility for paying the household ele ctricity bill, peak billing 
season, highest recorded electricity bill during peak season, number of appliances in use during 
peak consumption, and average monthly electricity bill. It also records the types of appliances 
frequently used at home and whether the respondents currently use any energy monitoring tool or 
system. These details provide essential context for analyzing household energy usage patterns and 
evaluating the potential impact of WisEnergy.

The survey data in Figure 92 shows the distribution of respondents according to their city 
of residence. Out of the total participants, 198 respondents (47.3%) are from Lapu-Lapu City, while 
220 respondents (52.7%) are from Mandaue City. This indicates a relatively balanced 
representation between the two areas, ensuring that the gathered data reflects the perspectives and

Type of Residence 
 
Figure 93: Respondents’ Type of Housing 
Figure 93 shows the distribution of respondents based on their type of residence. The 
majority, 235 respondents, live in houses, followed by 112 residing in subdivisions. Boarding 
houses account for 48 respondents, while apartments or condominiums represent the smallest group 
with 23 respondents. This breakdown highlights how living arrangements may influence household 
energy consumption and the applicability of the WisEnergy system. 
According to Ali et al. (2021), housing characteristics such as dwelling size and structure 
significantly affect electricity consumption, with larger homes typically using more energy due to 
a greater number of appliances. Similarly, Gupta et al. (2024) emphasized that household type and 
socio-economic conditions shape comfort needs and appliance usage patterns, especially in urban 
areas. These findings indicate that different residence types lead to distinct energy behaviors, where 
detached houses often have higher consumption compared to shared or rental units. Understanding 
these variations allows the WisEnergy system to be more adaptive and responsive to the unique 
energy management needs of each household type.

Bill Responsibility 
 
Figure 94: Household Electricity Bill Responsibility 
Figure 94 shows that 284 respondents are directly responsible for paying their household’s 
electricity bills, while 134 are not. This indicates that most participants are actively involved in 
managing energy expenses, giving them firsthand awareness of consumption patterns and billing 
fluctuations. Their direct role allows them to provide valuable insights into current energy habits 
and the potential benefits of using WisEnergy. 
Research shows that households directly engaged with their bills are more willing to adopt 
energy management systems (Chen et al., 2021) and seek more information about their energy use 
(Trotta, 2021). Likewise, consumers who experience significant bill in creases are more likely to 
invest in energy -saving measures (Lang et al., 2024). Thus, respondents who handle their own 
electricity payments are more likely to recognize changes in consumption and appreciate the 
usefulness of monitoring tools like WisEnergy. 
Peak Billing Season

Figure 95 presents the distribution of respondents based on the time of year when their 
electricity bills usually reach their highest amounts. The largest group, comprising 132 respondents, 
reported that their electricity bills remain consistent throughout the year , indicating stable 
consumption patterns. This is followed by 110 respondents who identified the holiday season 
(October–December) as their peak billing period, which may be attributed to increased appliance 
use for celebrations, gatherings, and festive li ghting. Another 100 respondents reported that the 
summer season (April –June) is when their bills peak, likely due to higher usage of cooling 
appliances such as air conditioners and electric fans.  
A smaller number of respondents indicated the rainy season (July–September) as their peak 
period (42), and the fewest respondents, 34, experienced peak bills at the start of the year (January–
March). These variations suggest that both seasonal climate changes and lifestyle patterns influence 
household electricity consumption. Studies have shown that residential cooling behavior varies 
significantly across seasons (Lyu et al., 2022) and that even small temperature increases can raise 
household electricity de mand (Roberts et al., 2022). Similarly, Ferrari et al. (2023) found that 
residential electricity loads typically peak during hotter months, supporting the observed summer 
increase in consumption among respondents.

households operate a substantial number of appliances simultaneously when consumption is at its

that the number of household appli ances and occupancy patterns significantly influence peak

Average Monthly Bill 
 
Figure 98: Common Monthly Electricity Bill Ranges of Respondents 
Figure 98 presents the distribution of respondents’ average monthly electricity bills, 
showing that the majority, 248 respondents, pay above ₱3,000 per month, followed by 79 
respondents within the ₱2,001 –₱3,000 range and 72 respondents paying ₱1,001 –₱2,000. Only a 
small portion reported lower bills, with 15 respondents paying ₱501 –₱1,000 and 4 respondents

Similar findings have been documented in international and local studies. Ali et al. (2021) 
identified that household electricity consumption increases with income, number of rooms, and 
appliance ownership, showing that higher living standards directly correlate with increased energy 
use. Never et al. (2022) further observed that middle -class households in the Philippines exhibit 
growing electricity demand influenced by behavior, environmental awareness, and appliance 
usage. Additionally, Olatunde, Okwandu,  and Akande (2024) highlighted that adopting energy -
efficient appliances significantly reduces household electricity consumption and overall expenses 
by minimizing energy wastage while maintaining performance.

Frequently Used Appliances 
 
Figure 99: Appliances Most Frequently Used by Respondents 
Figure 99 shows that electric fans (361) and refrigerators (359) are the most frequently 
used appliances, followed by washing machines (335) and televisions (316). Air conditioners (296) 
also have high usage despite their significant energy consumption. Desktop com puters (238), 
microwave/oven units (196), and water heaters (145) are used less frequently but still contribute to 
household electricity demand. 
These findings are consistent with studies showing that cooling and refrigeration 
appliances account for the largest share of household energy use. Ali et al. (2021) reported that 
kitchen and cooling equipment are dominant electricity consumers in urban ho useholds, strongly 
influenced by lifestyle and appliance ownership. Gupta et al. (2024) similarly found that household 
comfort needs—particularly thermal comfort—drive the frequent use of fans, refrigerators, and air 
conditioners across low- and middle-income housing. Moreover, Lyu et al. (2022) demonstrated 
that seasonal variation in residential cooling behavior substantially raises electricity demand, 
especially during warmer months. 
Together, these studies support the survey’s observation that frequently used appliances 
such as fans, refrigerators, washing machines, and air conditioners are major contributors to 
household electricity consumption. They also highlight the importance of WisEnergy’s appliance-
level monitoring feature to help users track high -consumption devices, adjust behavior, and 
promote energy-efficient practices.

Use of Existing Energy Monitoring Tools 
 
Figure 100: Respondents Currently Using an Energy Monitoring Tool or System 
Figure 100 shows that only 13 respondents currently use an energy monitoring tool or 
system at home, while the vast majority (405 respondents) reported not using one. This low 
adoption rate reveals a significant opportunity for WisEnergy to address the gap by offeri ng an 
accessible, user-friendly, and affordable platform for real-time energy tracking and management in 
households. The results indicate that most households still rely on traditional methods, such as 
manually checking monthly bills, which provide limited  insight into daily or appliance -level 
consumption. 
This finding is consistent with global research showing that the use of Home Energy 
Management Systems (HEMS) remains low, especially in developing countries. Chen, Gan, and 
Zhang (2021) found that many users are discouraged by the perceived complexity, hi gh upfront 
cost, and lack of awareness about potential benefits. Similarly, Trotta (2021) reported that 
households often underestimate their electricity usage and have little demand for energy feedback 
technologies due to limited understanding of how these  tools work. Lang, Nakolan, Rapson, and 
Taylor (2024) further observed that many households tend to respond only after receiving unusually 
high energy bills, rather than actively monitoring their consumption throughout the month. 
These patterns reflect a broader behavioral challenge in energy management: users often 
recognize the importance of saving electricity but lack practical tools that make tracking and 
analysis simple and rewarding. By integrating real -time monitoring, AI-driven recommendations, 
and intuitive mobile features, WisEnergy can bridge this awareness gap, motivating users to adopt 
more efficient habits and proactively manage their energy use.
